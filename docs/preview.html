The short version

One study. Four uncomfortable answers.

Most “best timeframe” claims compare backtests without costs, without a trial count, and without a final exam. This study adds all three. The result is less exciting and more useful.

01 / COSTS

Costs decide the timeframe.

The median 5-minute strategy had a positive gross Sharpe of +0.244 and a negative net Sharpe of −3.207. The signal did not disappear. The toll did.

02 / SELECTION

The winner is a warning.

The best trial reached 1.54 in-sample Sharpe and then fell to −0.50 on the holdout. The top ten produced three positive holdouts. None had a confidence interval that excluded zero.

03 / RISK

Overlays are risk controls.

Multi-timeframe filters and confluence improve average net Sharpe, but mostly by cutting volatility and turnover. Their gross alpha effect is small. Useful, but not magic.

04 / OPERATIONS

Your budget changes the answer.

Bitcoin preferred daily bars at every budget. Ethereum and Solana shifted toward faster bars when more trades were allowed. The right timeframe is the slowest one your decisions can support.

01

Lock the protocol

Pre-register the assets, timeframes, strategies, costs, hypotheses, and decision rule before opening the holdout.

02

Charge for reality

Apply 12 bp per unit of turnover, execute at the next bar’s open, and report net Sharpe alongside gross Sharpe.

03

Open the exam once

Evaluate 638 holdout days once. Count every trial. Deflate the winner. Report the losers and the limitations.

The winner’s confidence interval: the best trial’s OOS Sharpe was −0.498, with a 95% interval of [−2.094, 0.727]. The range comfortably includes “no edge.”

The rank-correlation trap: across all trials, IS and OOS Sharpe correlate at 0.709. Among in-sample winners, the correlation is −0.030. The overall number is driven by obviously bad strategies, not by skill among the contenders.

What this does not mean: a 15.6% PBO is not a clean bill of health. It is a warning that the winner is still a hypothesis.

The playbook

What to do Monday morning.

You do not need to reproduce the study to use its discipline. Ask better questions, charge real costs, and reserve a final exam.

01

Start with the budget.

How many decisions can you genuinely make and execute per week? Choose the slowest timeframe that preserves them.

02

Count the trials.

A winner from one test and a winner from 1,008 tests are different claims. The trial count belongs in the result.

03

Charge costs at the decision bar.

Be conservative about execution. A gross Sharpe is a marketing number; a net Sharpe is an operating number.

04

Write the protocol first.

Lock assets, timeframes, variants, costs, and the decision rule before you look at the holdout.

05

Open the exam once.

No peeking, no re-tuning, no “one more variant.” The holdout is only useful if it stays untouched.

06

Prefer risk controls to clever filters.

Lower volatility and turnover are more robust than another signal. Treat overlays as seatbelts, not engines.

If you allocate capital

  • Demand net returns and turnover.
  • Ask for the trial count and a deflation adjustment.
  • Ask what happened on the holdout.
  • Ask what would prove the thesis wrong.

If you build strategies

  • Pre-register before the holdout.
  • Report the distribution, not just the winner.
  • Test at two or three times your assumed cost.
  • Treat a large-grid winner as a hypothesis.

If you sell a strategy

  • Show the losers and the trial count.
  • Show drawdowns and capacity limits.
  • Explain the decision budget.
  • Publish the limitations. Trust is built there.
